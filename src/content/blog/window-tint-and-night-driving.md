Before choosing a tint, think about the darkest part of your usual drive. A side window that looks comfortable on a brightly lit road can make it harder to see an unlit kerb or a pedestrian as you turn into a driveway. Darker film reduces the light from a bright headlamp and from objects in shadow at the same time.

## Read the figure for the finished window

**Visible light transmission (VLT)** is the percentage of visible light that passes through the material tested. Higher VLT means more light reaches you. Find out whether the figure on a sample is for the film alone or for the film fitted to a particular type of glass. Your side windows may already have a factory tint.

For example, 3M’s Crystalline 20 data sheet gives **21%** visible light transmission on clear glass and **17%** on its automotive test glass for the same film. Ask the installer to measure your existing glass and explain the expected reading for the finished window. [3M Crystalline film data sheet](https://multimedia.3m.com/mws/media/1478566O/3m-crystalline-automotive.pdf)

## Compare the view you actually use

- **Side mirrors after dark.** With the car parked, look through a sample towards the mirror on the passenger side. Include a dim background. A brightly lit display inside a showroom tells you little about that view.
- **Parking ramps.** Look through the side glass at an unlit kerb or marking. Compare with the untreated glass from the same seat. If you lose detail, try a lighter option.
- **Heat during the day.** Compare the manufacturer’s solar-energy rejection figures alongside VLT. A darker appearance alone tells you little about how much heat different films reject.

## When glare is the problem

Start with clean glass inside and out. Check for smears, haze or a scratched windscreen while parked. If headlamps cause new or persistent halos, discuss that with an eye-care professional before making a film choice. A darker side window cannot address the source of glare directly ahead through the windscreen.
